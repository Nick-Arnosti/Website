One of the advantages of the clean analysis is that it is relatively straightforward to generalize.

The Prophet Secretary Problem

In order to get a better performance guarantee, we need to increase expected demand (lower the threshold \(t\)). The risk is that by doing so, we increase the chance of missing out on a large value.

Their analysis captured this through \(\delta_k(t,{\bf F})\)

\[ \alpha_k(t,{\bf F}) = \mathbb{E}_{V \sim {\bf F}} \left[\frac{\min(1+D_t(V),k)}{1+D_t(V)} \right].\] Note that \(\alpha_k(t,{\bf F}) \geq \delta_k(t,{\bf F})\), since the ratio inside of the expectation is \(1\) whenver \(D_t(V) < k\).

Proposition 1 In the random arrival order model, for all \({\bf F}\), \(k\), \(t\), we have \[ \frac{ST_k^t({\bf F})}{LP_k({\bf F})} \geq \min(\alpha_k(t,{\bf F}),\mu_k(t,{\bf F})).\]
To prove this, we fix \(t\) and \(V\), and define \(D^i = {\bf 1}(V_i > t)\), \(D = \sum_{i = 1}^n D^i\) and \(D^{-i} = D - D^i\). The following identity can be quickly verified by sequentially considering the cases \(D < k\) and \(D \geq k\): \[\begin{equation} \min(D,k) = \sum_i D^i \frac{\min(1 + D^{-i},k)}{1 + D^{-i}}. \tag{1} \end{equation}\]

When demand \(D\) is at most \(k\), then every value above \(t\) is selected. When \(D\) is above \(k\), then each value above \(t\) is selected with probability \(k/D\). From this observation, we can calculate the performance of any static threshold policy as follows.

\[\begin{align*} ST_k^t({\bf F})& = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i = 1}^n V_i D^i \frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}} \right] \\ & = \mathbb{E} \left[\sum_{i = 1}^n \left(tD^i + \max(V_i - t,0) \right) \frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}} \right] \\ & = t \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i = 1}^nD^i\frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}}\right] + \mathbb{E} \left[\sum_{i = 1}^n \max(V_i - t,0) \frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}} \right] \\ & = t \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ \min(D,k)\right] + \sum_{i = 1}^n \mathbb{E} \left[ \max(V_i - t,0) \right] \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{\min(1+D_t^{-i}(V),k)}{1+D_t^{-i}(V)} \right] \\ & \geq t \cdot \mathbb{E}\left[ \min(D,k)\right] + \sum_{i = 1}^n \mathbb{E} \left[ \max(V_i - t,0) \right] \alpha_k(t,{\bf F}) \\ & = t \cdot k \cdot \mu_k(t,{\bf F}) + U(t,{\bf F}) \alpha_k(t,{\bf F}). \end{align*}\]

We established in a previous post that the performance of the LP relaxation is at most \(t \cdot k + U(t,{\bf F})\), from which Proposition 1 follows.

Note that \(\mu_k\) is decreasing in \(t\), and \(\alpha_k\) is decreasing. This suggests a natural algorithm: choose a threshold \(t^M\) such that \(\mu_k(t,{\bf F}) = \alpha_k(t,{\bf F})\). We will also consider an even simpler algorithm: choose threshold \(t^{LP}\). Call these algorithms \(A^M\) and \(A^{LP}\), respectively. We start by analyzing \(A^{LP}\).

Lemma 1 For all \(k, {\bf F}\), we have \(\mu_k(t^{LP},{\bf F}) \geq \alpha_k(t^{LP},{\bf F})\).
Proof. By (1), we have \[\begin{align*} k \cdot \mu_k(t,{\bf F}) & = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_i D^i \frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}} \right] \\ & = \sum_i \mathbb{E}[D^i] \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{\min(1+D^{-i},k)}{1+D^{-i}}\right] \\ & \geq \sum_i \mathbb{E}[D^i] \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{\min(1+D,k)}{1+D}\right] \\ & = \sum_i (1 -F_i(t)) \alpha_k(t,{\bf F}). \end{align*}\] Therefore, when we choose \(t = t^{LP}\), we have \(\sum_i (1 -F_i(t)) = k\) and \(\mu_k \geq \alpha_k\).
From this, we conclude that the competitive ratio of algorithm \(A^{LP}\) is at least \[\begin{align*} \min & \mathbb{E}[\min(1,k/(1+D))] \\ s.t. & D = \sum_i D^{i} \\ & D^i \sim Ber(p_i) \\ & \sum_i p_i = k. \end{align*}\]

A result of Hoeffding (1956) implies that there is an optimal solution where each \(p_i\) takes on one of three values, two of which are \(0\) and \(1\). In other words, every non-degenerate \(D^i\) has equal \(p_i\). Thus, we can search over a two-dimensional family (number of \(i\) with \(p_i= 1\), number of \(i\) with \(0 < p_i < 1\)). The following code does this.

#Looking for minimum (or maxmimum) of E[f(N)], when N is sum of independent bernoullis.
#By Hoeffding (1956), can reduce to two-parameter search (all probs that are not 0 or 1 are equal)
compute_value = function(f){
    return(Vectorize(function(lambda,k,n_ones,n_others){
        if(n_ones > lambda){ print("ERROR: INFEASIBLE (n_ones too large)"); return(NA)} #must have n_ones <= lambda 
        if(n_ones+n_others < lambda) {print("ERROR: INFEASIBLE (n_others too small)");  return(NA)} #Can't spread lambda mass among so few
        if(n_others == 0){ return(f(n_ones))}
        N = c(0:n_others)
        p = (lambda-n_ones)/n_others #probability of success for "others" 
        return(sum(dbinom(N,n_others,p)*f(N+n_ones)))
    }))
}
alpha = Vectorize(function(N){return(min(1,k/(1+N)))})
mu = Vectorize(function(N){return(min(1,N/k))})
k = 2
lambda = k
n_ones = c(0:floor(lambda))
n_total = ceiling(lambda) + c(0:10)
A = matrix(1,nrow=length(n_ones),ncol=length(n_total)) #A[i,j] will give \alpha with n_ones[i] ones and n_total[j] - n_ones[i] others
M = matrix(1,nrow=length(n_ones),ncol=length(n_total)) #M[i,j] will give \alpha with n_ones[i] ones and n_total[j] - n_ones[i] others
for(i in 1:length(n_ones)){
    A[i,] = compute_value(alpha)(lambda,k,n_ones[i],n_total-n_ones[i]) 
    M[i,] = compute_value(mu)(lambda,k,n_ones[i],n_total-n_ones[i]) 
}
pmin(A,M)

For \(k \geq 5\), it appears that the worst case is when there are no variables with \(p_i = 1\), and many variables with \(0 < p_i < 1\). For smaller \(k\), the worst case of this optimization problem involves some variables with \(p_i = 1\). For example, with \(k = 1\) the worst case is \(1/2\), and with \(k = 2\) it is \(2/3\). These observations lead to the following conjecture.

Conjecture 1 For \(k \geq 5\), algorithm \(A^{LP}\) offers a guarantee of \(\mathbb{E}[\min(P^{k},k)]/k\).

For \(k < 5\), algorithm \(A^{LP}\) offers a guarantee of \(k/(k+1)\).

How about our original algorithm? Based on playing around with small \(k\), I conjecture the following.

Conjecture 2 Algorithm \(A^M\) offers performance guarantee of \(\mathbb{E}[\min(P^{k},k)]/k\)

Unfortunately, here we can't immediately apply the results of Hoeffding (1956).
