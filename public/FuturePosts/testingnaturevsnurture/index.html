#Simple Example

Low-achieving and high-achieving students, low-achieving students mostly apply to school B. School assignment has no effect on achievement. Low achieving get an average of 16, while high-achieving get an average of 24.

Each high-achieving attends A with probability 90%, and B with probability 10%.

Each low-achieving attends A with probability 10%, and B with probability 90%.

Because school A mostly consists of high-achieving students, it’s average ACT score is \[\frac{90 \times \color{red}{24} + 10 \times \color{red}{16}}{90+10} = 23.2\]

\[\frac{10 \times \color{red}{24} + 90 \times \color{red}{16}}{10+90} = 16.8\]

Where do these probabilities come from? And what if there are differences we don’t observe? For example, some low-income students are actually high-achieving, but those students are disproportionately likely to go to school A.

#Slightly More General Example

Suppose that there are two schools, A and B. Each can accept 100 students.

There are 240 students in the district. 180 of them rank A ahead of B, while the remaining 60 rank B ahead of A. To decide who to admit, A and B use (independent) lotteries.

School \(A\) has a lottery cutoff of \(1/2\), and school \(B\) has a lottery cutoff of \(1/3\) (i.e. a \(2/3\) chance of acceptance).

For each student \(i\), we observe their school \(s_i \in {A,B}\) and their test score \(t_i\).

Assume that a student’s test score depends only on their characteristics and school assignment, and not on others’ assignment. (Ideally, could separate peer effects from school effects.)

For school \(A\): each student who lists \(A \succ B\) gets a weight of 2, while those who list \(B \succ A\) get a weight of \(6\) (3 times greater).
