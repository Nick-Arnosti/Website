Building on Akbarpour Li Gharan

Nick Arnosti

2024/05/29

 

Introduction

Arrivals at rate \(m\)

Departures at rate \(1\)

Probability of compatibility \(p = d/m\).

If we fix \(d\), loss doesn’t really depend on \(m\).

Suppose \(m = d\), so \(p = 1\). Then loss of greedy is \(1/(2d+1)\). (And presumably greedy is optimal, since loss is exactly expected pool size divided by \(m\).)

Let \(S\) be the pool size (in steady state), divided by \(m\). Then by PASTA, the probability of matching upon arrival is \(1 - (1 - d/m)^{mS}\). Since every match formed by greedy involves one new arrival and one waiting agent, the total fraction of agents that match is \(2\mathbb{E}[1 - (1 - d/m)^{mS}]\). But because we have exponential departures, the fraction of agents that depart must be equal to \(\mathbb{E}[S]\). Thus, the steady-state distribution of \(S\) under greedy must satisfy \[2\mathbb{E}[1 - (1 - d/m)^{mS}] + \mathbb{E}[S] = 1\] \(2\mathbb{E}[1 - e^{-m \cdot log(1-d/m)S}] + \mathbb{E}[S] = 1\)

By concavity, we have \(2(1 - e^{-m \cdot log(1-d/m)\mathbb{E}[S]}) + \mathbb{E}[S] \ge 1.\) Thus, \(\mathbb{E}[S]\) is at leas the solution \(x\) to \(2(1-e^{-m \cdot log(1-d/m)x})+x=1\). This lower bound is increasing in \(m\).

So this doesn’t quite get us that loss is increasing in \(m\) (though simulations suggest that is correct). But we can analyze \(m = d\) and \(m \rightarrow \infty\).

Claim Suppose lifetimes are exponential with mean 1, and fix \(d > 0\). When \(m = d\), loss of greedy is \(1/(2d+1)\). As \(m \rightarrow \infty\), loss of greedy converges to solution \(x\) to \(2(1 - e^{-dx}) + x = 1\).

Natural thing to do is to compare these two bounds to each other.

Now, let’s bound loss of any online algorithm. Again, let \(S\) be the steady state normalized pool size.

On the one hand, loss is at least \(\mathbb{E}[S]\). On the other, loss is at least \(\mathbb{E}[(1-d/m)^{mS}]/(d+1)\), as this is the probability that a new arrival (i) doesn’t have anyone compatible in the pool upon arrival, and (ii) doesn’t have anyone compatible show up before they depart. Noting that \(\mathbb{E}[(1-d/m)^{mS}] \ge (1-d/m)^{m\mathbb{E}[S]}\), we conclude that if \(\mathbb{E}[S] = x\), loss is at least \(\max(x,(1-d/m)^{mx})\). A-L-G do some work to show that the solution \(x\) is always at least \(1/(2d+1)\). This should show that greedy is in fact optimal when \(p = 1\).
