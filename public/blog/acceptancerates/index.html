A recent blog post by Al Roth highlighted an article by Samuel Abrams explaining that statistics from the New York High School Match are often presented in a misleading way. This post will give my take on this topic, and discuss several alternative statistics.

#The New York High School Match

Each year, eighth graders are asked to submit a ranked list of up to twelve schools. Then an algorithm takes these lists and generates an assignment, and students are told which school they will attend.

Often, journalists calculate an “acceptance rate” by dividing the number of seats at a school by its number of applicants (students who list it).

https://www.nytimes.com/2017/03/10/nyregion/new-york-city-high-school-admissions-rate.html

To illustrate why these numbers are misleading, let’s consider a simple example. Suppose that there are 10 schools, each with 100 seats, and 1,000 students, each of whom lists all 10 schools.

  • If all students rank school 1 first, school 2 second, and so on, then all schools will have identical “acceptance rates” of 10%, even though school 1 is clearly the most competitive. If school 10 had only 99 seats, then it would have the lowest “acceptance rate”!

  • Another absurdity rises if each school is listed first by 100 of the 1,000 students, and has 101 seats. Then every school has an “acceptance rate” of 10.1% (101/1000), despite the fact that it fails to fill its seats and would take any student who wants to attend.

The fundamental problem is that many students who listed a school could have gotten in, but end up at another school that they prefer. However, because the match is centralized (unlike college admissions), students are never officially accepted to schools ranked below their assigned school.

#Two Alternatives

Each student who lists a school falls into one quadrant of a 2 \(\times\) 2 grid, shown in Figure 1.

Figure 1: Categorization of Applicants.

  • Some students will get into another school that they prefer, while others will not. Only the latter group is truly “interested” in attending.

  • Some students will have high enough priority to get in, and others will not. Everyone in the former group is “admitted”, regardless of whether they end up enrolling.

The flawed measure discussed above is Enrolled/Applied.

In his article, Samuel Abrams suggests “adding the number of students who matched with a particular school to the number of students who matched with a school they ranked lower than that school and then dividing the number of matches by that sum.” In other words, he proposes using the ratio

Selectivity = Enrolled/Interested.

Another option is to define Acceptance Rate = Admitted/Applied.
